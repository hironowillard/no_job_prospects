Selected Work
Scenario-Based eLearning

From Delivery to Discussion

Role Instructional Designer / eLearning Developer
Format Articulate Rise · 8 lessons · ~60 min
Audience New facilitators, first six months

Overview

From Delivery to Discussion is a scenario-based onboarding module for new workshop facilitators. It teaches a four-phase framework: Engage, Structure, Challenge, Apply. Facilitators learn to recognise which phase a live session has entered, and what each phase costs when it gets skipped.

The module follows three facilitators. Marcus is warm, experienced, and well-liked, and his workshops change nothing. Marjane does the work Marcus skips. Irene has fifteen years of experience and repeats Marcus's mistake beneath a layer of competence. Learners meet all three in sequence, and the diagnosis grows harder each time. Marcus takes five minutes to read. Irene takes the whole module.

The module closes with Your Day with Irene, a five-part scenario set across one working day. Learners shadow a senior facilitator through a pre-session walkthrough, a mid-session judgement call, a planning brief, a debrief, and one last question in a hallway. Four correct out of five to pass. No one fails it. Some people just get to learn it twice.

Instructional Challenge

Facilitator training typically teaches a model, confirms the model has been understood, and returns people to rooms where they work exactly as they did before. Learners can name the framework and still cannot use it. Recognising which phase a live session has entered is a different skill from understanding the sequence on paper, and only one of them transfers to the job.

The failure this module addresses is also invisible from inside. A facilitator who over-delivers receives warm feedback, engaged rooms, and strong evaluation forms. Nothing in the environment signals a problem. Marcus is competent, which is precisely what makes him difficult to learn from, and framing his failure as a skills gap would teach the wrong lesson entirely.

This module was designed to withhold its own framework until learners have felt the problem it solves. It opens inside Marcus's workshop and asks learners to notice something is wrong before supplying the vocabulary to name it. The design mirrors the content. Learners arrive at the conclusion themselves, which is exactly what the module asks facilitators to let their own rooms do.

Design Approach

  • Narrative spine across three recurring facilitators, each embodying a distinct failure mode
  • Framework withheld until the learner has felt the problem it solves
  • Escalating diagnostic difficulty, from obvious failure to subtle failure to the same failure disguised as expertise
  • Every distractor written to be plausible and instructive, with feedback on wrong answers that teaches
  • Remediation routing that returns the learner to the specific section a miss came from
  • Flashcards contrasting how each facilitator would handle the same moment
  • A full worked session plan, annotated phase by phase, with the three ways new facilitators break it
  • Self-review checklist and FAQ as post-session job aids, designed to survive outside the module
On the scenario

The assessment runs as a single working day, told in five timestamped moments. Learners read rooms they only shadowed and produce judgements they could defend to the person who hired them. The final question turns on a principal in a grey blazer who said nothing and left first, and on what that silence was evidence of.

One question, in full

The first of the five, reproduced exactly as a learner meets it. The module locks the Diagnostic behind the four sections that make it answerable, so it is reprinted here instead.

Question 01 of 05 · maps to Section 1

8:47 AM. The pre-session walkthrough.

You're standing in the back of a room that's still being set up. Irene is at the front, talking through her plan for the morning session with two other facilitators. You're listening.

The session is for nine middle school principals. Topic: handling difficult parent conversations. Irene has been asked to deliver this session four times this year. Today is the fifth.

You hear her say to the other facilitators: "I've got this one dialed in. The opening story works every time, the framework slides are clean, and the role-play at the end always lands. We should be done fifteen minutes early."

The other facilitators nod. One says, "Glad it's you and not me."

She hasn't mentioned who's in the room today.

What is the most important thing you've just observed?
  • AIrene is well-prepared and confident. This is a sign of an experienced facilitator running a session she's mastered.
  • BIrene is describing a session she has stopped designing and started performing. The phrase "works every time" and the assumption of finishing early both signal she's on autopilot, which is the same pattern as Marcus, just dressed in fifteen years of competence instead of nine.
  • CIrene is being efficient. Topics that get repeated should run faster as the facilitator improves; that's not a problem, it's expertise.
  • DThe other facilitators' reactions suggest the session topic is genuinely difficult, which means Irene's confidence is reassuring and appropriate.
Feedback if they choose B

Right. And notice how hard this one is to see, because Irene is not Marcus. She's not warm and quotable; she's senior and competent and running a session she's run four times. But the pattern underneath is identical. "Works every time" is a sentence about delivery, not about learners. The assumption of finishing early means the session has been pre-engineered to a known outcome, which leaves no room for what these specific nine principals actually bring into the room. Irene might still run a fine session today. But the warning signs are the same ones you learned to spot in the first section of this module, just quieter.

Feedback if they choose A

Confidence and preparation are good. "Works every time" is the tell that this isn't preparation; it's repetition. A facilitator who's still designing the session asks who's in the room today. A facilitator on autopilot assumes the room is the same as the last four. The first kind learns. The second kind plateaus.

A miss here routes the learner back to Section 1, Why the Framework Exists, not to the start of the module. Each of the five questions maps to the section that answers it. Four correct out of five to pass, unlimited retries, and the Close stays locked until the learner gets there.

Module Structure

Eight lessons. The first half is observation, watching other people's rooms. The second half shifts the work onto the learner, who plans a session, self-assesses, and finally reads a day they only shadowed.

01 Introduction
Marcus's workshop, watched from the back row, before any framework is introduced
A hand going down; a whisper; something off the learner cannot yet name
02 Why the Framework Exists
Delivery as the universal default when facilitators are tired, rushed, or unsure
Lineage in Kolb's experiential cycle, inquiry-based instruction, and constructivist learning theory
Diagnosing Marcus from the evidence in a first knowledge check
03 The Four Phases
What each of the four phases is for, and what it costs to skip one
Flashcards contrasting Marcus and Marjane at the same decision point
Sequence over checklist, and what a session running only one phase actually produces
04 Planning a Session
A real-shaped brief for sixty minutes with fifteen teachers who believe they already do this well
A full annotated session plan with deliberate, unequal phase proportions
The three ways new facilitators break a plan, from equal phases to padded Structure to optional Challenge
05 Applying It Independently
A post-session self-review checklist, one honest question per phase
The questions new facilitators actually ask, answered without reassurance
06 Your Day with Irene
One working day, shadowing the facilitator who hired you
Five moments where reading the room correctly is the entire job
07 The Diagnostic
Five scenario questions, timestamped across the day, four to pass
Misses route back to the section they came from
08 Close
Back to Marcus's room, and what the learner can now name about it
Sherwood Anderson, and what it means to be a peddler of words

Try It Yourself

The full module, running live. It takes about an hour end to end, though the first two lessons are enough to see the design argument at work. The framework stays withheld while Marcus delivers a workshop that everyone in the room enjoys and nobody is changed by.

Lessons unlock in sequence, and the Close sits behind a passing score on the Diagnostic. The gating is deliberate: the assessment only asks a fair question once the four sections have done their work. If you have five minutes rather than an hour, the Introduction and the section that follows it carry the whole argument.

Learning Outcomes

What facilitators develop
  • Facilitators can name the four phases a session moves through and explain what each is doing for the learner
  • Learners recognise when a session is stuck in one phase too long, including when the room feels good
  • Participants can proportion a session against the specific trap contained in a brief
  • Facilitators develop the harder habit of reading silence, ease, and warm feedback as data
